Sage Control Dossiers — Methodology v0.1

Who can move your money, how fast, and how cheaply can that power be captured?

A Sage dossier answers the off-chain half of due diligence that bytecode scanners cannot: who holds which keys, what they can and cannot do, how governance works, what has gone wrong before, and whether the protocol is still alive. It is meant to be read by an agent in a few hundred milliseconds and by a human in five minutes.

1. Evidence rules

Every claim carries a confidence tag and at least one sources id.

TagMeaning
primary-verifiedI read the primary source myself (source code, official docs, official repo)
secondary-reportedReported by news or a governance mirror; primary not read in full
unverifiedPlausible but not checked; listed so the gap is visible

Anything I could not verify goes in unknowns. An unknown is never scored as zero. A null score means "not assessed", and the dossier says so.

2. Five scores (0 = low concern, 3 = high concern)

U — Upgradeability

  • 0: no proxy / immutable code
  • 1: upgradeable only through governance with a timelock of 7 days or more
  • 2: upgradeable through governance with a shorter timelock, or a multisig behind a timelock
  • 3: upgradeable by an EOA or multisig with no timelock

A — Admin power over users (what the strongest role can do if it acts in bad faith)

  • 0: cannot affect principal or yield
  • 1: can only change fees within a hard on-chain cap, or add new options
  • 2: can pause, redirect flows (fees, emissions), or freeze, but not seize principal
  • 3: can move principal, or swap oracles, collateral or admin on existing positions

G — Governance capture

  • 0: no token governance over user funds
  • 1: token governance with lock-ups or a vetoer, plus a meaningful timelock
  • 2: token-denominated quorum with short delays, or a documented attempted capture in the same design family
  • 3: a successful capture within 12 months, or a governance-attack cost below 1% of value at risk

L — Lifecycle

  • 0: actively maintained
  • 1: governance or infrastructure migration in progress (configuration-change risk)
  • 2: merger or sunset announced; legacy contracts losing support
  • 3: deprecated or abandoned

I — Incidents (last 12 months)

  • 0: none verified (absence of evidence is not evidence of absence)
  • 1: minor (no user loss)
  • 2: one material incident (loss or bad debt under $5M), or an attack with unknown outcome
  • 3: several material incidents, or a loss above $5M

attention = the worst dimension, labelled in words, with the reason.

3. Governance Attack Cost (GAC)

GAC is the dollar cost of buying enough votes to pass a proposal on your own, before slippage.

GAC_lower_bound_usd = max(quorum_votes, opposing_votes + 1) * token_price_usd
leverage            = value_at_risk_usd / GAC_lower_bound_usd

Why it matters: most DAOs set quorum as a number of tokens. When the token price falls 95%, the cost of capture falls 95% too, while the value the governor controls (TVL) may not fall at all. The March 2026 Moonwell Moonriver case shows the extreme: about $1,800 of MFAM, roughly a quorum-sized position, was reported to put about $1.08M of user funds at risk. That is about 600x leverage.

What lowers real-world risk even when GAC is low:

  • Voting delay (time between proposal and the vote-weight snapshot): a long delay stops "buy, propose, vote" in one sitting. Moonwell documents 1 minute.
  • Timelock before execution, which gives users and guardians time to react.
  • Guardian / vetoer roles that can cancel malicious proposals (these add trust in the guardian).
  • Vote-escrow locks (ve-tokens), which make votes cost time as well as money.

GAC is a lower bound. It ignores slippage (which raises the cost), and it also ignores borrowing or renting votes (which can lower it).

4. What a dossier is not

It is not an audit, not investment advice, and not a safety rating of any specific market or vault. It is a structured, sourced map of control, dated as_of, meant to be re-checked. Core-contract dossiers explicitly exclude vault and oracle choices, which often dominate losses.
